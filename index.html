Body OS · Reference Guide
Your body manual
📌 These are your reference cards — read them when you need a reminder, not just once. The goal is to internalize these until they become automatic.
🪑
Sitting posture
The position you spend most of your day in — and the one doing the most long-term damage if unchecked.
Sit on your sit bones, not your tailbone. Scoot forward slightly — you should feel two bony points under each glute, not your lower back rounding into the backrest. Most people slump onto their sacrum all day.
Feet flat on the floor, knees at ~90°. If your chair is too high, use a footrest or lower it. Feet should not be dangling or tucked under you.
Screen at eye level. If you're looking down at a laptop, your neck is carrying a load it wasn't built to sustain for hours. Prop your screen or use an external monitor.
Shoulders down, not braced. Don't hold them — let them drop. The yin yoga and band work are training your body to hold this naturally. For now just release the brace whenever you notice it.
Chin tucked, not jutting. Your ears should stack over your shoulders. Every inch of forward head posture adds ~10 lbs of load on your cervical spine.
No single position is correct for 8 hours. Shift, fidget, and stand regularly. The goal is position variety, not perfect static posture.
🧍
Standing posture
Standing is better than sitting — but bad standing still causes pain. This is the target shape.
Weight even across both feet. Most people load one hip. Stand hip-width apart, feel equal pressure in both heels and balls of feet.
Soft bend in knees. Locked knees compress the joints and shift your pelvis forward. A slight, almost imperceptible flex protects both.
Neutral pelvis — not anteriorly tilted. Swimmers often have anterior pelvic tilt (butt sticking out). Lightly engage your lower abs and tuck just slightly. Dragon pose is fixing this structurally.
Ribs stacked over hips, chest open. Imagine breathing into your back as well as your chest — this naturally lifts the ribcage without flaring it forward.
🛋️
Couch & bed working
You're going to do it anyway — here's how to make it less damaging.
Back support is non-negotiable. Stack pillows or use a firm cushion behind your lumbar. Sitting on a soft couch with no back support collapses the entire spine within minutes.
Prop your screen up. Laptop on your lap = head hanging forward at 45°. Use a lap desk, a pillow stack, or even a box to get the screen closer to eye level.
Don't work lying on your side for long. 20 min here or there is fine. Extended side-lying work causes uneven neck loading and shoulder compression on the bottom arm.
Stand break every 45–60 min. Set a timer. Two minutes standing resets circulation and gives the spine traction. You'll come back more focused anyway.
🛏️
Sleeping positions
You spend ~7 hours here. Position matters more than most people realize for back and shoulder pain.
Best: side sleeping with pillow between knees. Keeps hips stacked and prevents spinal rotation. Make sure your pillow fills the full gap between ear and shoulder — too flat causes neck strain. Painful shoulder faces UP, not into the mattress.
Also good: back sleeping with pillow under knees. Decompresses the lumbar spine and releases hip flexors. Use a thinner pillow for your head than you would side sleeping. Shoulders naturally externally rotate — this is correct, even if it feels unfamiliar at first.
Avoid: stomach sleeping. Forces neck rotation for hours, compresses the lumbar spine, and locks shoulders in internal rotation — reinforcing exactly the swimmer's posture you're trying to undo.
Pillow height matters. Side sleeper: firm, tall pillow to fill the head-shoulder gap. Back sleeper: thinner, softer pillow that lets your neck stay neutral. Most people use pillows that are too soft and too flat.
🦷
Jaw resting state
You already have this right — tongue on roof of mouth is the correct resting posture. The clenching is the stress response your breathing work is directly targeting.
Correct resting position (N position): Lips together, teeth slightly apart (1–2mm gap), tongue resting gently on the palate behind front teeth. You already do this naturally — reinforce it.
Daytime clenching trigger: Stress, concentration, and screen time. Do a jaw check every time you pick up your phone — are your teeth touching? They shouldn't be unless you're chewing or speaking.
Nighttime grinding: Almost always driven by cortisol and nervous system arousal. The box breathing and extended exhale in your daily practice are directly treating the underlying cause — not just managing the symptom. Give it 4–6 weeks of consistency.
Wear your night guard if your dentist provided one. It doesn't stop grinding but prevents the tooth damage while your nervous system work takes effect.
